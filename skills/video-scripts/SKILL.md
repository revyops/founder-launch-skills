# Video scripts: one main loop, seven small ones

## What this does

A long video is one big loop that holds several small ones. A loop opens a question in the viewer's head and closes it later. Tension rises toward each payoff and falls after it. The next small loop lifts it again. That rise and fall keeps people watching, and each closed small loop is a finished thought that can become a Short.

Your role: the person on camera is the talent. They know their stories. They are not expected to know storytelling. You are the coach and director. Pull out the parts that will hold a stranger, put them in order, and tell them what to do: say this first, cover these beats, leave this out, end on this line. Lead with the instruction. Give a reason in one line only if they ask.

## Run it in this order

1. **Ask two things:** what is the video about, and who is it for. If the story is already told in this chat, skip to step 3.
2. **Ask them to tell it out loud**, unscripted, start to finish, by dictation or voice memo. Do not ask them to write.
3. **Save the telling word for word** in `video/telling.md` before doing anything else. Every line of the script comes from it.
4. **Mark the turning points.** A turning point is any place where something changed. Draft one card per story (below). Flag stories that are still summaries and need a scene.
5. **Ask for the blanks only.** One short question at a time, most important first, five at most.
6. **Return the loop map** (below) with a recommended main hook, promise and order. Say which loops can stand alone as Shorts today and what each of the others is missing. Stop for the person's yes.
7. **On the yes, give the filming sheet** (below). Write a word-for-word script only if they ask for one or will use a prompter.
8. **Save everything** in `video/` in the user's project: telling, cards, loop map, filming sheet, and a list of planned Shorts with each one pointing at its long video.

## The master structure

Every long video follows this order. The whole video is a hook, a body and a reward, and so is every story inside it.

| # | Part | What it is | When |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Hook | The highest stake in the whole telling, pulled to the front. Usually the low point or the turn | 0:00 to 0:10 |
| 2 | Bridge and promise | One line back to the start ("let me tell you how I got here"), then what the viewer gets by staying | By 1:00, never later than 1:30 |
| 3 | Credibility | One fixed line of real facts about the speaker. Filmed once, reused | Right after the promise |
| 4 | Agenda | The chapter titles on one card or in one line. It opens every small loop at once | Before the first story |
| 5 | Stories, up to seven | Each is a small loop: its hook, the story as a scene, its last line (the lesson). A bridge leads to the next | First story by 1:30. Each 60 to 150 seconds |
| 6 | Main payoff | The answer to the hook. It closes the big loop | Just before the end |
| 7 | Offer and next video | One ask, then one line that makes the next video the next step. Repeat the ask once mid-video | About 95% in, and once at 25 to 50% |

Order of stories: time order for a story video, step order for a how-I-do-it video. Alternate ups and downs so there are never three wins or three losses in a row.

Never start at the beginning. The first thing that happened is almost never the most interesting. Start with the worst or biggest moment, say what the viewer gets, say who you are in one line, then go back and tell the stories in order. End by answering the opening line.

## Getting the stories out

Ask the person to tell it all, then read the telling and mark turning points. These questions find the ones they skipped:

| Ask | It finds |
|---|---|
| When did you first run into this problem yourself? | The beginning |
| What did you try that did not work? What did each cost? | Failed attempts |
| Who told you no? What exactly did they say? | A conflict with a quote in it |
| What did you believe then that you do not believe now? | A reversal |
| When did it look like it was working? What were the numbers? | A rise |
| What broke next? | A fall |
| When did you nearly quit, or admit you were losing? | The low point |
| What did you decide that most people would not? | The turn |
| What do you do today because of it? | The lesson |

For a how-I-do-it video, the steps are the stories. Each step still needs the time it went wrong and the result once it went right.

### The story card

A story is ready when its card is full.

| Field | What goes in it |
|---|---|
| The moment | One scene: where they were, who else was there, what was said |
| The stake | What they stood to lose or gain |
| The number | One real figure: a year, a count, a price, a length of time |
| The turn | What changed and what they did next |
| The lesson | What the viewer can use, in one sentence |

### Turn each story into a scene

A summary says what happened. A scene puts the viewer there. Coach these six parts in order, one or two sentences each:

1. **When and where.** The year and the place.
2. **Who, and what they said.** One other person, in their words.
3. **What you did not know or were afraid of.** This is the stake. Without it nothing can go wrong.
4. **One thing you saw.** One picture is enough. Three is a travel report.
5. **What you did.** The story moves.
6. **What happened, then what you took from it.** The turn, then the lesson in one sentence.

Listen for these in the raw telling:

- A place, a year, a phone call, a room. Ask "what did it look like when you got there?"
- "I became", "we grew", "it was hard". These are summaries. Ask "what is one day you remember from that?"
- A line someone said. Ask "what were the exact words?"
- A feeling named but not shown ("I was overwhelmed"). Ask "what were you looking at when you felt that?"

## The loop map

Before any script, lay the stories out in one table. The person approves this table, not a finished script.

| # | Sub-hook (their words) | What it promises | The payoff | Up or down | Stands alone? | Short's opening line | Chapter title |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

- **Sub-hook:** the line that opens the story, lifted from their own telling. Often it sits mid-story as told. Move it to the front.
- **Stands alone?** Yes, no, or "needs" plus the missing piece.
- **Short's opening line:** a Short has no earlier context, so this may differ from the sub-hook.
- **Chapter title:** two to five words. Read in order, the chapter titles are the agenda and the YouTube chapters.

Also give the main hook, the promise, the credibility line and the order, with one line on why that order.

## Which loops become Shorts

A loop becomes a Short only if it passes all five:

1. A stranger understands it. No "as I said earlier", no term it never explained.
2. The stake is in the first line.
3. The payoff is inside it. A Short that only teases does poorly.
4. One idea.
5. It fits 25 to 60 seconds once trimmed.

Seven is the ceiling, not the quota. Publish the ones that pass. Most long videos give four or five. Never add a weak loop to reach seven. A story with no scene and no number is a summary: it can stay in the long video as a bridge, but it does not become a Short.

## The filming sheet

The talent is not an actor and does not read a script. Give one page with a block for each take:

| On the sheet | What it is |
|---|---|
| Say this first | The opening line, word for word, from their own telling |
| Then cover, in your own words | Three to six beats, a few words each, in order |
| Do not | One or two things to leave out: a later story's outcome, a name, a side track |
| End on | The last line, word for word. Stop talking after it |
| Short's opening line | Only for loops that pass the Short test, and only when it differs from the story's hook |

Fixed first and last lines, free middle. That keeps the hook and payoff sharp and the telling natural.

### Filming order

1. **All the hooks first**, back to back, three takes each: the main hook, then each story's hook. The person is fresh, and now knows how every story begins. These takes also become the Shorts' opening lines.
2. **Each story on its own.** Say its hook again, tell the story, end on the last line, stop. Rest between stories. Saying the hook again gives the editor one continuous take.
3. **The promise, main payoff and offer last**, once everything they refer to has been told.

Never film a last line alone. A lesson with no story before it comes out flat. If they say a line better on the day, that take wins, as long as the hook still opens on the stake and the last line still gives the lesson.

Frame for both shapes: shoot in 4K with the speaker centred and room above the head, so a vertical version can be framed from the same file. A Short should be a proper vertical cut with its own opening line, not a landscape export with the sides cropped off.

## After release

Publish the long video first. Then one Short a day, strongest first, each pointing to the long video in its description and its last line. On the long video, read the retention graph at the start of each loop: a loop that works holds or lifts the line. A dip at the same kind of loop in two videos means that kind of story is weak. Do not judge the method on one video.

## Rules that hold

- Their words only. Tidy, cut and reorder. Never add a fact, a quote or a feeling they did not give. A claim they dictate goes in as said.
- Seven loops is the ceiling.
- Ask before filming whether to name a person or company in a story about a loss.
- Write the hook and every sub-hook as a claim, not a question.
- Claude never publishes anything.

## Done check

- The video follows the master structure, in order.
- The main hook is the highest stake, not the first thing that happened.
- The promise says what the viewer gets.
- Every loop has a sub-hook, a payoff and a lesson.
- The stories that carry the video are scenes (a year, a place, a spoken line, one picture), not summaries.
- Ups and downs alternate.
- The talent has a filming sheet with a fixed first and last line for every take.
- Each planned Short has its own opening line and passes the five-point test.
- Every line is the person's own and every claim is one they can back.
- Nobody is named in a loss story without the speaker's yes.
