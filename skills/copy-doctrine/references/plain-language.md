# Plain language and cutting words

## Target

Sixth to seventh grade reading level, and lower where it costs nothing. The aim is not to dumb it down. A reader who has to translate is a reader you are losing.

## The two tests

**The peer test, for jargon.** Keep a term only if the audience uses it when talking to a peer, not when explaining to their boss. A sales leader says "CRM" and "sequence" to another sales leader, so both stay. Nobody says "de-anonymization" to a peer, so it goes.

**The translation test, for everything else.** After writing a line, ask: if someone asked what this means, would I explain it in different, simpler words? If yes, use those words. The explanation is nearly always clearer, because you already stripped out the performance.

## Translate, do not delete

Some jargon carries the argument. "Encrypted" is the whole case against "stored". You cannot cut it, so say it plainly.

> Before: End-to-end cryptographic protection across the data lifecycle.
> After: Your files are encrypted, so only you can read them.

Same claim, many grades lower, and the difference from the alternative survives.

## Three traps that survive plain words

- **Shorthand that assumes decoding.** "She owns the number" sounds insider-smart and says less than "she is responsible for hitting the sales target."
- **Coined phrases that label instead of describe.** Friction, bandwidth, visibility gap, adoption curve. Each names a thing. The reader has to unpack the name first.
- **Drama where a direct statement works.** "Works great on day one, then slowly falls apart" is a story arc. "It breaks because nobody has time to maintain it" lands faster.

## The readability trap

Splitting a long sentence to hit a reading level often makes a choppy pair that needs a bridge added back. Always re-read after splitting. Connect with *because, so, but, which means*.

## Spelling

Pick one standard and use it everywhere, including chart labels and image captions. One stray spelling from the other standard usually hides in a chart label nobody checked.

## Cutting words

Use as few words as possible while staying simple, complete and clear.

**Cut:** repetitive, redundant, unnecessary, verbose, complicated or fancy words.
**Never cut:** a claim, a number or a differentiator.

### The five kinds of fat, in the order to hunt them

1. **Said twice in adjacent elements.** The biggest source, and the easiest to miss because the two statements are never in the same paragraph. A headline says it and the body restates it. A card title says it and the card body repeats it. A section intro lists four conditions and the closing block lists the same four.
2. **Said twice in one paragraph.** Two sentences making the same claim in different words. Delete one.
3. **Words carrying no information.** *at any moment, at once, over time, for good, really, very, actually, in order to, the fact that, at the end of the day.* "The points fade over time" becomes "the points fade".
4. **Setup a payoff does not need.** A sentence whose only job is to introduce the next one.
5. **Lists that appear in full elsewhere.** If a grid below has the eight categories, the paragraph above does not need them.

### Translation is not compression

They are opposite operations, and people confuse them.

- Jargon-dense sections stay the same length or grow. Replacing five technical nouns with plain descriptions costs words.
- Genuinely verbose sections shrink by a fifth to a half.
- Fragment-built sections grow. "Label, dash, description" bullets become sentences, and a sentence costs more than a fragment. They read far better.

Diagnose which of the three you are looking at before editing. It decides whether "make it shorter" is even the right instruction.

### What compression breaks

Cutting removes antecedents. If you cut "The 26 ship on day one." then "You can add more." loses its noun. Always re-run the pronoun check after cutting.

### Two tests before shipping a cut

- Would a reader actually stumble? A dangling pronoun whose noun sits an inch above on the same screen is not a defect.
- Does it change what someone understands, or only how it reads? If only how it reads, it is a preference, and preferences do not justify another round of edits.
