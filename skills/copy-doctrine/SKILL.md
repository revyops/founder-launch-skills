# Copy doctrine: plain English, no AI tells

## What this does

It makes copy sound like a person wrote it and a busy reader can follow it. It has two jobs, and they are kept apart:

- **Writing.** Load the rules before drafting, so the copy comes out right the first time.
- **Checking.** Run finished copy through the rules to find what drifted. Do not ask "does this read right" and accept a full rewrite. Report what you found, then fix it.

## When to read the extra files

Read only what the task needs.

- `references/ai-tells.md`: the long detection list. Read it when auditing, or when a draft feels smooth but empty.
- `references/plain-language.md`: reading level, the jargon tests and a swap table. Read it when copy is technical or jargon-heavy, and for cutting words.
- `references/surfaces-and-voice.md`: how the same voice changes for a website, email, video, LinkedIn post, reply or DM, plus how to write in the user's own voice. Read it for any channel other than a web page, and for anything posted under a person's name.
- `references/examples.md`: before and after edits. Read it when you need to see a rule applied.

## Steps

1. Find out the job: write new copy, or audit existing copy. Find out the surface (page, email, post, reply, script) and the reader.
2. Collect the facts you may use. Never add a claim, number, customer or result the user has not given you.
3. For new copy, draft from the user's own words if they exist (notes, a transcript, a voice memo). Clean them up a little. Do not rewrite them into tidier sentences.
4. Run the five passes below, in order.
5. Run the five-second audit. Fix, then run pass 1 again.
6. Hand back the copy. For an audit, list what you changed and why, in a few lines.

## The five passes (order matters)

Fixing one layer breaks another. Removing jargon creates coined phrases. Cutting words creates dangling pronouns. Tightening rhythm brings stacking back.

1. **Tells.** Em dashes, demonstrative openers, vague pronouns, forced contrasts, rhetorical questions. Mechanical. No judgement.
2. **Plain.** Translate jargon into what the thing does. This costs words.
3. **Cut.** Remove what repeats.
4. **Voice.** Does it sound like a person, and like this person?
5. **Re-check.** Run pass 1 again. Passes 2 and 3 always create new violations. Skipping this pass is how copy ships broken.

Run the passes as a list, not by feel. For every line, name the noun each pronoun points at. Scan for banned openers as strings. Count em dashes.

Stop after about four rounds. After that the changes go sideways, and each new edit risks more than it fixes.

## The twelve rules (each has its exception)

A rule without an exception gets misapplied. The point of every rule is to stop a reader stumbling. Where nobody would stumble, leave the line alone.

**1. No em dashes.** In prose, use a full stop, comma, colon or middle dot. Keep en dashes in number ranges (21-27 months). Em dashes are the loudest tell and the cheapest to fix.

**2. No demonstrative openers.** No sentence starts with *That, That's, Those, This, These, Here's, There's*, or with *It* pointing back at the last sentence. They glue sentences together with a pronoun, and they usually label something you just described ("That's a scoring problem"), which sounds like insight and adds none. Exception: fixed idioms such as "If that's you, we'll say so on the first call."

**3. Name the thing, don't point at it.** A pronoun needs exactly one obvious antecedent, in the same sentence or the one before. Failure modes: the noun is two or more sentences back, or two nouns compete. Exception: where the answer is on the same screen (a line saying "You can add more" under a heading that names what to add) nobody stumbles, so leave it.

**4. No forced negation.** "It's not X, it's Y." "Not a tool, a system." "Instead of guessing, you know." Spend at most two per page, on the sharpest. Exception: a section whose purpose is refusal ("Things we won't claim") is built on negation. It is the content, so it does not count.

**5. The contrast test.** A contrast earns its place only when both hold: the reader cannot be assumed to know the default, and the setup states a concrete fact rather than a characterization. "Most agents run on a schedule. Yours runs when a score crosses your line" passes. "Most platforms say they do X. We do it better" fails.

**6. No parallel construction for rhythm.** Repeated openers ("each worth more, each fading faster"), fragment couplets ("No model to tune, nothing to drift"), imperative pairs ("See it. Then act on it") and triads ("fragmented, duplicated, undated"). If you can hear a rhythm, a machine wrote it. Exception: a repeated short word used as a plain list of concrete items, such as three separate "No ..." grievances, each with its own detail.

**7. No rhetorical or leading questions.** "Ever wonder why your pipeline stalls?" "Sound familiar?" Ask nothing the reader did not ask. State the thing. A real question to a real person, where you want the answer, is fine.

**8. Describe, don't coin.** When you remove jargon, do not invent a plainer-sounding compound ("fade rate", "visibility gap", "friction"). Say what the thing does in a normal sentence. A label has to be decoded. A description explains.

**9. Three to four word minimum.** No one- or two-word sentences for drama ("Wrong." "Settled."). Join short clauses with *so, which means, because, but*.

**10. Connect ideas in prose. Allow stacking in cards.** In paragraphs, link sentences with *because, so, but, which means* so the reader follows the logic. Exceptions: card and list copy built for scanning, and sequential beats that each stand alone ("A visit, then a pricing page, then a new VP, then a reply. Any one means nothing. Together they are obvious.").

**11. Concrete over abstract.** Use the number, the timeline or the name when you have one. "Working product in two weeks" beats "fast turnaround". "Six months" beats "two quarters", because nobody thinks in quarters outside a board deck.

**12. No hype, no flattery.** No *world-class, industry-leading, game-changing, revolutionary, best-in-class*. If you have a real ranking, metric or award, name it. If not, drop the adjective. Praise for the reader only with a specific reason.

## Plain language in one paragraph

Aim for a sixth to seventh grade reading level, and lower where it costs nothing. Keep a jargon term only if the audience says it to a peer, not only when explaining to their boss. After writing a line, ask whether you would explain it in different, simpler words if someone asked what it meant. If yes, use those words. Use one spelling standard (US or UK) throughout, including chart labels and captions. More in `references/plain-language.md`.

## Cutting in one paragraph

Cut what is repetitive, redundant, verbose or fancy. Never cut a claim, a number or what makes the offer different. Fat hides in this order: said twice in adjacent elements (headline and the body under it), said twice in one paragraph, filler words (*really, very, in order to, the fact that*), setup the payoff does not need, and lists that appear in full elsewhere on the page. Translation is not compression: replacing jargon usually keeps the length or grows it. After any cut, re-check pronouns, because cutting removes the nouns they pointed at.

## The five-second audit

Run before anything ships.

- Any em dashes in prose?
- Any paragraph opening with *Moreover, Furthermore, That said, Additionally*?
- Any *delve, unpack, underscore, leverage, synergy, holistic*?
- Any sentence starting with *That, That's, Those, This, These, Here's, There's*?
- Does any pronoun have two possible antecedents, or one more than a sentence away?
- Any bold-word-colon-explanation bullet block?
- Any rhetorical or leading question?
- Any one- or two-word sentence?
- Any triad where two items would do?
- Could the closing line close any memo on earth? Cut it.
- Read each paragraph aloud. Can you state its point in one plain sentence? If not, delete it.
- Any noun stack of three or more, any label-colon-recipe, any maxim used as a punchline?
- The talk test: would the writer say each sentence out loud, in those words, to a friend?

## What holds on every surface

No em dashes. No demonstrative openers. Every pronoun has one obvious antecedent. No coined phrases. No rhetorical questions. Concrete over abstract. Nothing that cannot be backed up. Everything else bends with the channel.

## Rules for the writer (you)

- Never write a claim the user could not defend on a call with a skeptical buyer. If you cannot back it, leave it out, however good it sounds.
- Never paste a topic into yourself and produce a finished piece from nothing. The user's rough notes beat a polished draft built from air.
- When the user's own wording breaks a rule on purpose, keep their wording and say so.
- Do not use this on advice or conversation with the user. It governs copy.

## Done-check

- All five passes ran, and pass 1 ran again after pass 3.
- The five-second audit is clean.
- Every claim, number and name came from the user.
- The copy passes the talk test.
- For an audit: you listed what changed and why, and kept the user's phrasing wherever it was not a violation.
